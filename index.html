1. The physical picture

Fluid of speed $U$ and kinematic viscosity $\nu$ meets a cylinder of diameter $D$. The no-slip condition forms a boundary layer on the surface. On the rear half the pressure rises along the flow, and when the layer can no longer overcome that adverse gradient it separates and forms a wake. The Reynolds number $\mathrm{Re}=UD/\nu$ decides how that wake looks, because it measures inertial forces (which carry and stretch vorticity) against viscous forces (which smooth it).

2. Governing equations and the Reynolds number

Derivation: Navier-Stokes and nondimensionalization

For incompressible flow of constant density $\rho$ and viscosity $\nu$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf u}{\partial t}+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.$$

Scale lengths by $D$, velocities by $U$, time by $D/U$ and pressure by $\rho U^2$: $\mathbf x^*=\mathbf x/D$, $\mathbf u^*=\mathbf u/U$, $t^*=tU/D$, $p^*=p/\rho U^2$. Every term then carries a factor $U^2/D$ except the viscous one, which carries $\nu U/D^2$. Dividing through:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf u^*}{\partial t^*}+(\mathbf u^*\cdot\nabla^*)\mathbf u^*=-\nabla^*p^*+\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}\nabla^{*2}\mathbf u^*,\qquad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{UD}{\nu}.$$

The no-slip condition $\mathbf u^*=0$ on the cylinder and uniform $\mathbf u^*=\hat{\mathbf x}$ far upstream add no other parameters, so geometrically similar flows with equal Re are the same flow.

3. Drag coefficient

Derivation: drag from pressure and shear

The force on the cylinder comes from pressure and viscous shear on its surface $S$ (length $L$ along the axis):

$$F_D=\oint_S\big(-p\,\mathbf n+\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf n\big)\cdot\hat{\mathbf x}\,dS,\qquad C_D=\frac{F_D}{\tfrac12\rho U^2DL}.$$

Splitting $C_D=C_{D,p}+C_{D,f}$ shows that friction is a large share at low Re, while above a few hundred pressure drag from the low-pressure wake dominates. The drag crisis is a sudden drop in $C_{D,p}$ caused by delayed separation.

4. Vortex shedding and the Strouhal number

Derivation: why St depends only on Re

The shedding frequency $f$ can depend only on $\rho,U,D,\mu$: five variables in three base dimensions (M, L, T). The Buckingham Pi theorem gives two independent groups:

$$\mathrm{St}=\frac{fD}{U}=\mathrm{St}(\mathrm{Re}),\qquad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho UD}{\mu}.$$

For a smooth cylinder St rises from about 0.12 at onset to about 0.2 and stays near that over the subcritical range, so $f\approx0.2\,U/D$.

5. How the live simulator works

The simulator is a D2Q9 lattice Boltzmann solver with BGK collision. In lattice units the viscosity is $\nu=(\tau-\tfrac12)/3$, so with the inlet speed $U=0.12$ and a cylinder $D=16$ cells wide, the relaxation time is set from the slider by $\tau=\tfrac12+3UD/\mathrm{Re}$ (about 0.519 at Re = 300). The inlet imposes equilibrium at speed $U$, the outlet copies the neighboring column, the top and bottom wrap around, and the cylinder uses half-way bounce-back.

Limitations and a sanity check

The model is 2D, the lattice Mach number is about 0.2, the circle is stair-stepped, and the periodic walls give about 1/8 blockage. In headless tests the flow stayed steady at Re = 20 and shed periodically at Re = 100 with St ≈ 0.18, a little above the ≈ 0.165 expected for an unbounded cylinder, which is plausible given the blockage.

Regime tour

Boundaries are approximate and shift with free-stream turbulence, surface roughness and end conditions. Each button loads a representative Re in the App tab.

Where this matters

Chimneys, risers and cables. When the shedding frequency approaches a structural natural frequency, the alternating side force drives vortex-induced vibration. Helical strakes and fairings disrupt coherent shedding.

Worked example. Air at 5 m/s past a 0.2 m pipe ($\nu\approx1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s) gives $\mathrm{Re}\approx6.7\times10^4$, in the subcritical range, so $f\approx0.2\times5/0.2=5$ Hz.

Bridges. The 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse is often cited as vortex shedding, but engineering analyses attribute it to self-excited aeroelastic flutter. Shedding does matter for slender hangers and stay cables.

Sports balls. Golf-ball dimples trigger boundary-layer transition at lower Re, producing the same drag-crisis mechanism on a sphere.
