What the Reynolds number measures

For a cylinder of diameter $D$ in a stream of speed $U$ and kinematic viscosity $\nu$, the Reynolds number is $\mathrm{Re}=UD/\nu$. It compares inertial forces, which carry the flow past the body, with viscous forces, which smooth velocity differences. Small Re means viscosity wins; large Re means inertia wins and the flow becomes unstable.

Derivation: Navier-Stokes and nondimensionalization

For incompressible flow of constant density $\rho$ and viscosity $\nu$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf u}{\partial t}+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.$$

Scale lengths by $D$, velocities by $U$, time by $D/U$ and pressure by $\rho U^2$: $\mathbf x^*=\mathbf x/D$, $\mathbf u^*=\mathbf u/U$, $t^*=tU/D$, $p^*=p/\rho U^2$. Substituting, every term in the momentum equation carries a factor $U^2/D$ except the viscous one, which carries $\nu U/D^2$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf u^*}{\partial t^*}+(\mathbf u^*\cdot\nabla^*)\mathbf u^*=-\nabla^*p^*+\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}\nabla^{*2}\mathbf u^*,\qquad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{UD}{\nu}.$$

The no-slip condition $\mathbf u^*=0$ on the cylinder and uniform $\mathbf u^*=\hat{\mathbf x}$ far upstream contain no other parameters. Geometrically similar flows with equal Re are therefore the same flow.

Drag and shedding versus Re

The plots follow the slider. Curves are approximate values from the literature (see the data file header for sources), not output of the simulator.

The drag crisis near Re ≈ 3×10⁵ occurs because the boundary layer turns turbulent before separating, which delays separation and narrows the wake.

Derivation: drag coefficient

The force on the cylinder comes from pressure and viscous shear on its surface $S$:

$$F_D=\oint_S\big(-p\,\mathbf n+\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf n\big)\cdot\hat{\mathbf x}\,dS,\qquad C_D=\frac{F_D}{\tfrac12\rho U^2DL}.$$

Splitting $C_D=C_{D,p}+C_{D,f}$ shows that at low Re friction contributes a large share, while for Re above a few hundred pressure drag from the low-pressure wake dominates. The drag crisis is a change in $C_{D,p}$ caused by where the boundary layer separates.

Derivation: Strouhal number

The shedding frequency $f$ can depend only on $\rho, U, D, \mu$. That is five variables in three base dimensions (M, L, T), so the Buckingham Pi theorem gives two independent groups:

$$\mathrm{St}=\frac{fD}{U}=\mathrm{St}(\mathrm{Re}),\qquad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho UD}{\mu}.$$

For a smooth cylinder St rises from about 0.12 at onset to about 0.2 and stays near that value over the subcritical range, so $f\approx 0.2\,U/D$.

Where this matters

Chimneys, risers and cables. When the shedding frequency approaches a structural natural frequency, the alternating side force causes vortex-induced vibration. Helical strakes and fairings disrupt coherent shedding.

Worked example. Air at 5 m/s past a 0.2 m pipe ($\nu\approx1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s) gives $\mathrm{Re}\approx6.7\times10^4$, in the subcritical range, so $f\approx0.2\times5/0.2=5$ Hz.

Bridges. The 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse is often cited as vortex shedding, but engineering analyses attribute it to self-excited aeroelastic flutter. Shedding does matter for slender hangers and stay cables.

Sports balls. Golf-ball dimples trigger boundary-layer transition at lower Re, producing the same drag-crisis mechanism on a sphere.
